Every long-running home game accumulates its own house rules, but most of them get invented on the fly and never actually get explained right — someone half-remembers a rule from a cardroom or a broadcast final table, everyone at the table nods along, and the mechanic gets applied a little wrong for years. Here are five worth knowing properly instead of guessing at.

## Bomb Pot

Everyone antes a larger-than-usual amount, preflop betting gets skipped entirely, and the flop comes out straight away with betting starting from whoever's first left of the button. It's not a separate game, it's a modifier you drop into a normal session to shake up a stretch of card-dead hands. The appeal is structural: skipping preflop means nobody folds a marginal hand before seeing three community cards, so bomb pots tend to see more players to the flop and bigger, weirder pots than a normal hand does. Good for the stretch of a session where everyone's been folding pocket fours preflop and the table's gone quiet.

## Big Blind Ante

Instead of every player anteing individually — real chip-counting friction in a home game, especially with a mixed denomination stack — just the big blind posts one ante that covers the whole table. A short stack who can't cover both the blind and the ante pays the blind first. It's the same total money into the pot as a full round of individual antes, just collected in one motion instead of eight. If your game still does antes the old way, this is a pure quality-of-life upgrade with no real downside.

## Ties and the Odd Chip

The actual mechanical answer to a question every home game eventually argues about: when a pot splits and doesn't divide evenly, who gets the extra chip? Default answer — it goes to the worst-positioned player relative to the button, not the best. In a stud-shaped game, a tie on the deciding card gets broken by suit rank instead (spades, then hearts, diamonds, clubs) rather than position. In a hi/lo split specifically, the high hand gets the odd chip, not the low hand. And in a hybrid split — a five-card hand against a four-card Badugi hand, say, or a draw hand against an Omaha hand — the side holding the bigger, more-card hand gets treated as "high" for this purpose specifically, even though that's not how the actual pot value gets split. Worth writing down somewhere visible so it stops being a five-minute argument every time it comes up.

## The Bad-Roll Penalty

Relevant if your table ever runs one of the dice-decided variants — a die roll setting a low-qualifier, or deciding whether a hand plays high-only or splits. The house rule worth stealing isn't the game itself, it's the enforcement around a bad roll: the die has to travel far enough across the table and actually tumble, not just get nudged and called good — a dealer's judgment call, same idea as a dart that clips the wall instead of the board. A bad roll costs the roller a small penalty, and the penalty doubles each time the same player rolls bad again in the same session. It's small money, but it keeps the game moving without a five-minute negotiation every time somebody flicks the die two inches.

## Jam-or-Fold

For anyone running an actual structured tournament rather than a cash session: a simplified action format where the only two options are shove all-in or fold, no in-between sizing at all. It sounds like it flattens the game, but it speeds up a home tournament considerably and strips out almost all the bet-sizing tells that trip up players who aren't used to thinking about sizing in the first place. Worth trying for the late, short-stacked stretch of a structured night rather than the whole thing.

None of these are hard to add. The actual work is agreeing on them once, out loud, before the money's already on the table — the games that run clean for years are the ones where somebody wrote the rule down instead of re-litigating it every session.
