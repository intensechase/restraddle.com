Almost every piece of poker strategy content you'll find is written for a stranger across the table. Population reads, GTO baselines, "most players in this spot do X" — all of it assumes you've never seen this person before and never will again. That's a reasonable assumption for a casino cash game or an online table. It's a completely wrong assumption for your home game.

You've played with the same five or six people for months, maybe years. That's not a smaller sample size problem to work around — it's a different game with a real edge built into it, and almost nothing gets written about how to actually use it.

## The book you're already building

You don't need a HUD or a notebook to have a read on your regulars. You already know things a population-stats approach can't tell you: who tightens up for an hour after losing a big pot, who only three-bets bluffs when they're stuck, who slowplays big pairs specifically because they got burned doing it the aggressive way once. None of that shows up in a solver. All of it shows up over a dozen sessions with the same faces.

The mistake is treating this knowledge as background flavor instead of an actual input to your decisions. If you know someone's continuation-bet frequency drops off a cliff when they've missed the board, that's not trivia — that's a fold-to-nothing spot you can attack every single week until they adjust, if they ever do.

## Reads decay slower than you'd think, but they do decay

The flip side of a long sample is that people change, and a read from eight months ago can actively mislead you if you're not updating it. Someone who used to never bluff river might have read a book, watched some streams, started three-barreling. The home-game version of this mistake isn't "I don't have enough information" — it's "I'm working off information that used to be true." Revisit your reads the same way you'd revisit a range assumption that's stopped paying off.

## They're reading you too

This part's easy to forget because it's less fun to think about. If you're tracking your regulars' tendencies, at least a couple of them are tracking yours — and unlike a stranger, they have just as much time and just as much motivation to build an accurate picture. Whatever edge you're building on table dynamics is being built on you as well. The players who actually profit long-term from this dynamic aren't the ones with the best reads; they're the ones willing to deliberately break their own patterns often enough that a read on them stays wrong.

## The uncomfortable part

There's a real social dimension here worth naming directly: these are your friends, and "exploit tendencies" is a strange phrase to apply to people you like. Most home games handle this fine because the stakes are low enough that it stays fun for everyone, and because the same dynamic runs in both directions — you're not extracting from a mark, you're playing a genuinely competitive game against people who know you back. If a game ever stops feeling that way, that's a real problem worth addressing directly rather than a strategy question.

Casino strategy content will never help you with any of this, because it's written for a table that resets every time you sit down. Yours doesn't. That's the whole advantage — use it.
