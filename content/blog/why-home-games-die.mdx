Ask around and you'll find the same pattern everywhere: a home game starts strong, runs great for six months to a year, and then just quietly stops. Nobody calls it off officially. The group text goes quiet, someone half-heartedly asks "we still doing this?" a few weeks later, and eventually everyone accepts it's over. Almost nobody blames the poker itself. They're right not to — the actual causes are almost always structural, and every one of them is fixable if you catch it early.

## The host is doing unpaid, invisible labor

Someone is sending the reminder text, buying the chips that need replacing, cleaning up after, dealing with the one guy who always wants to run it back an hour past when everyone else is ready to leave. If that's one person every single week, that person eventually burns out — not because they stopped liking poker, but because hosting is a real job nobody thanked them for after month four. The fix isn't complicated: rotate hosting, or explicitly split the logistics (someone else handles the reminder text, someone else handles chips) even if the game stays at the same house.

## Recruiting fatigue

Games don't stay at eight players forever. People move, have kids, get busy, lose interest. A game with no plan for replacing players slowly shrinks until it's not worth running — five committed players is a real game, three is a phone call to see if anyone's free. The games that last have someone (often, again, the host) treating "who's the next person we'd invite" as an ongoing question, not a crisis response after the game's already down to four.

## An unresolved money or blood imbalance

Every long-running home game has a version of this: one player who's up enough, consistently enough, that it's started to feel less like friendly competition and more like a wealth transfer. This doesn't have to end the game, but it will if nobody addresses it directly — either through a stakes conversation, a structural change (bringing in new blood so it's not the same five people transferring the same money back and forth), or just an honest acknowledgment that the skill gap is real and the group is fine with that. What kills games is the silent version, where everyone privately notices and nobody says anything until someone just stops showing up.

## No stated norms

The games that last tend to have unwritten rules that are actually agreed on, even if never written down: what stakes, what variants, what happens with IOUs, how late is too late to still start a new hand. The games that die tend to have a slow accumulation of small unresolved disagreements about exactly this stuff — not big blowups, just friction that never gets named and slowly makes the night less fun than it used to be.

## What actually keeps a game alive

None of this is about the cards. A home game is a small, self-organizing social institution, and like any institution, it survives on the boring stuff: someone owning logistics without burning out, a plan for replacing players who leave, and enough honesty about money and norms that small friction doesn't calcify into the reason everyone quietly stops coming. Get those three things right and the poker takes care of itself.
